# Event 02: ESPN Long-Form Interview at Fred Smith Field

## Status

Confirmed media canon.

## Format and setting

Approximately three weeks after Daniel Sandberg's introductory press conference, ESPN's Rece Davis conducts a long-form interview with him at the still-empty Fred Smith Field.

The visual framing emphasizes the scale and strangeness of the project: a completed major stadium, enormous institutional backing and no team yet playing inside it.

## Building before coaching

Sandberg admits that the role does not fully feel like coaching yet. Much of his work is organizational construction, hiring and program design. He enjoys parts of the business-building process, but is increasingly eager to return to football.

He describes feeling both hope and anxiety, while relying heavily on experienced FedEx operational and business leaders.

## Resources as obligation

Sandberg rejects the idea that elite facilities should create entitlement. Players entering the program will be told that the resources must be earned through preparation, conduct and performance.

His central message is that privilege creates obligation.

## Fear and reputational risk

Sandberg openly admits that he fears losing, embarrassing the university and turning the project into a national laughingstock.

He jokes that the Fred Smith family might hang him for treason if he fails badly enough.

He also identifies the project's central reputational paradox:

- if FedEx fails, critics will call the institution foolish
- if FedEx succeeds, critics will claim the company simply purchased success

Sandberg accepts that the only meaningful response is to build something substantial enough to survive that argument.

## Leadership and loneliness

Asked whether he is prepared for the loneliness of head coaching, Sandberg answers honestly: no, and probably nobody is.

He intends to protect himself from isolation by staying close to his family, friends, coordinators and staff. He describes a college coach as becoming a kind of father to young players who are still developing into adults.

His idealized version of the role is partly "Ted Lasso": empathetic, relational and group-oriented.

## Honesty as care

Sandberg rejects the idea that caring for players requires avoiding hard decisions.

He argues that telling a player he is not good enough for the team can be an act of care when it is delivered honestly and respectfully. Cruelty lies in humiliation, not in truthful evaluation.

He distinguishes between:

- "You are not good enough yet"
- "You are not good enough"

Sandberg acknowledges that coaches regularly misjudge talent. Players may need maturity, a mental breakthrough or a mechanical correction. Sometimes coaches simply miss.

When a player ultimately has no place on the roster, Sandberg wants the conversation to preserve dignity: explain the situation, provide time to improve when possible, then help the player find another opportunity and invite him to prove the evaluation wrong.

## Coaching role models

Sandberg identifies two major leadership influences.

### Sven-Göran Eriksson

Eriksson represents calm authority, dignity and respect without performative anger. Sandberg notes that Eriksson managed enormous international football egos without yelling or humiliating players.

### Sean McVay

McVay represents football intelligence, memory, innovation, drive and an unusually early commitment to coaching. Sandberg has met him twice and describes him as an exceptionally impressive person.

Sandberg wants to combine Eriksson's human leadership with McVay's command of football.

## A Nordic leadership model

Sandberg says he wants to be "less American" in the way he leads, while making clear that he does not mean less competitive.

He describes a Scandinavian or Nordic model with:

- less theatrical authority
- less yelling for its own sake
- more discussion
- mutual respect
- trust
- calm accountability

He acknowledges that some moments may require greater harshness, but rejects the assumption that American players inherently need more aggressive treatment than elite players elsewhere.

## Authority and credibility

Sandberg does not believe he enters the locker room with no authority.

His initial credibility comes from:

- his own NFL playing career
- his experience coaching at the college level
- his work as an offensive coordinator
- his football knowledge
- the respect he intends to show players

He does not claim that this creates permanent loyalty. It creates enough credibility to begin a relationship that must then be reinforced through reciprocal respect and competent leadership.

## The imagined first game

Asked when FedEx University will finally feel real, Sandberg describes a proposed national-anthem flyover by a FedEx cargo aircraft.

The spectacle will be followed by the opening kickoff, the first real hit and the first play call through the headset.

That sequence represents the moment when the facilities, branding, interviews and corporate experiment become an actual football team.

## ESPN closing narration

Rece Davis closes the segment by observing that Sandberg cannot yet prove FedEx University belongs in college football. The program has no roster, record or history.

What it has is belief, unusual honesty and time to turn both into a team.

The final line is:

> The first delivery is scheduled for 2026.
